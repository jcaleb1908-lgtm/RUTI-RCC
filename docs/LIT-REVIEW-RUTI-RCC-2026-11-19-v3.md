Prepared for: cohort study in the NIH All of Us Research Program (EHR
data) on whether kidney cancer risk after recurrent urinary tract
infection (UTI) reflects the infection, the antibiotics used to treat
it, or both, and whether the relationship differs in Hispanic / Puerto
Rican participants.

Search completed: 19 November 2026. Language: English. All numbers are
copied as published; items we could not verify are marked \[NOT
CONFIRMED\]. Text labelled **Our interpretation** is ours, not the
authors'.

# 1. One-page summary

## What is known

- **UTI and later kidney cancer: mixed and mostly short-term.** Older
  case-control studies disagree: Iowa OR 1.9 (1.5, 2.5) for
  self-reported kidney/bladder infection (Parker 2004); kidney infection
  RR 1.2, borderline (international RCC study, Schlehofer 1996);
  multiple cystitis episodes OR 1.60 (0.95–2.70) (Talamini 1990); lower
  UTI history linked to a reduced RCC risk in New South Wales (McCredie
  1992). Taiwanese claims cohorts report large HRs (UTI → urinary tract
  cancer aHR 4.66, 3.55–6.10, Sun 2013; kidney aHRs 2.90–5.20,
  Huang 2019) but with short follow-up and no lag.

- **Recurrent UTI has now been studied once for kidney cancer.** In
  42,377 Danish patients with hospital-coded recurrent cystitis
  (Mortensen 2026), the kidney cancer SIR was 3.84 (2.78–5.18) in year
  1, 1.27 (0.68–2.17) in year 2 and 1.24 (0.96–1.58) beyond 2 years
  (women 1.39, 0.96–1.95). In Sweden, kidney cancer SIRs after acute
  cystitis were 11.28 (9.67–13.09, men) and 7.72 (6.60–8.99, women)
  within 3 months and 1.05 (0.90–1.21, men) and 1.38 (1.25–1.51, women)
  after 5 years (Jansåker 2025). UTI prescriptions rise about 9 months
  before renal cancer diagnosis (Funston 2024).

- **Antibiotics and kidney cancer: modest, consistent associations.**
  Korea (Park, 2024 preprint) aHR 1.24 (1.12–1.36) for ≥365 antibiotic
  days vs non-use, 1.21 (1.08–1.37) after a 5-year washout; UK THIN
  (Boursi 2015) AOR 1.6 (1.1–2.2) for \>5 penicillin courses;
  meta-analysis OR 1.28 (1.1–1.5) for RCC (Petrelli 2019); UK Biobank
  childhood antibiotic use HR 1.48 (1.08–2.02) (Liu 2026) and 1.38
  (1.05–1.82) (Peng 2026). A 2026 umbrella review rated kidney cancer
  ‘highly suggestive’ (Xu 2026).

- **Detection bias is demonstrable in All of Us itself:** UTI and other
  diagnoses were followed by a short-term excess of diagnoses of an
  unrelated outcome (dementia) in All of Us and UK Biobank (Wang 2025).

- **Validated building blocks:** outpatient UTI codes PPV 84.9%
  (Germanos 2020); US claims UTI algorithms (single code: sensitivity
  \>72.1%, PPV \<78.3%; Fortin 2022); a US EHR recurrent-UTI definition
  (Ackerson 2024, not chart-validated). No validated US EHR algorithm
  for incident RCC was found; RCC records agreed \>70% across English
  registry, primary-care and hospital data (Kruithof 2026).

## What is not known

- Whether antibiotics or the infection drive the association: no
  kidney-cancer study compared antibiotic **classes within the same
  infection** or separated urinary from non-urinary indications.

- Long-term RCC risk after recurrent UTI against an **internal
  comparison group**, adjusted for smoking, obesity, stones and CKD,
  outside hospital-diagnosed patients (Mortensen compared with the
  general population and had no antibiotic or lifestyle data).

- Any result by **Hispanic ethnicity or Puerto Rican origin**; no US
  multi-ethnic EHR study of either link.

## What our study could add

- An All of Us cohort combining recurrent UTI (cystitis and
  pyelonephritis separately), class-specific cumulative antibiotics
  linked to indication, a 2-year lag/latency exclusion, active
  comparators and an imaging-intensity adjustment; plus exploratory
  ethnicity-stratified estimates (likely underpowered, Section 8).

**Verdict:** the combined design has not been done, to our knowledge,
after structured PubMed (E-utilities), PROSPERO and OSF searches
(Section 12; Embase and Google Scholar not searched). Nearest: Mortensen
2026 (recurrent cystitis, no antibiotics), Huang 2019 and the Korean
preprint. One related PROSPERO review is ongoing (CRD420251084288).

## 2.3 Studies that tried to separate infection from antibiotic

- **Huang 2019** varied antibiotic duration within UTI patients (≥14 vs
  1–6 days: men aHR 1.14, 0.37–3.54; women 1.73, 0.58–5.20) and compared
  UTI with pneumonia; no lag, imprecise.

- **Park 2024** adjusted for six infection categories and compared heavy
  with light users; no indication data.

- **Boursi 2015** adjusted urinary-tract cancers for prior UTIs
  (bladder, prostate models), used antivirals/antifungals as
  negative-control exposures and a 1-year lag.

- **Design analogue (prostate, not kidney): Russell 2018** analysed
  lower UTI and UTI antibiotics separately; associations appeared only
  6–12 months before diagnosis (UTI OR 1.50, 1.23–1.82; first antibiotic
  1.96, 1.71–2.25), while ≥10 antibiotics was associated with lower risk
  (0.85, 0.78–0.91); authors attributed this to detection bias.
  **Pottegård 2020** (bladder, Denmark) used UTI-antibiotic
  prescriptions with a 2-year lag (as described by Mortensen 2026).

**Our interpretation:** no kidney study separates the two. The cleanest
contrasts available in EHR data are recurrent-UTI vs single-UTI
patients, antibiotic class vs class within the same urinary indication,
and non-urinary antibiotics in people without UTI.

## 2.4 All of Us studies

No All of Us study of UTI or antibiotics and kidney cancer was found.
Relevant All of Us work: cancer ascertainment from EHR vs survey
(Aschebrook-Kilfoy 2022: 1,266 kidney cancers in EHR, 487 by survey, 313
in both); 2,215 RCC cases identified from EHR in version 8 (Agrawal
2026); kidney-cancer risk prediction from SNOMED codes (Lee 2025, AUROC
0.799); detection bias after UTI and other diagnoses (Wang 2025);
antibiotic exposure studies (Gu 2023, Gilmore 2023); air quality and
cancer risk (Craver 2024).

## 2.5 Systematic reviews, meta-analyses and registered protocols

- **Petrelli 2019:** antibiotics and RCC pooled OR 1.28 (1.1–1.5). **Xu
  2026** umbrella review: kidney cancer among ‘highly suggestive’
  associations (pooled figure \[NOT CONFIRMED\]).

- **PROSPERO CRD420251084288** (Goddard, Ahmed, Sanyaolu; registered 7
  July 2025, ongoing): UTIs and urological cancer (incl. kidney) in
  women; cohort/case-control; ROBINS-E. No matching publication found in
  PubMed.

- Other PROSPERO hits (45 for UTI/antibiotic × kidney cancer) concerned
  perioperative prophylaxis or antibiotics with immunotherapy. OSF
  registrations: none relevant (title search).

- Kidney stones → kidney cancer meta-analyses: RR 1.76 (1.24–2.49)
  (Cheungpasitporn 2015); RR 2.36 (1.74–2.98) (Bhojani 2026).

# 5. Biases and how earlier studies handled them

## 5.1 Detection (surveillance) bias

More than half of RCCs are now found incidentally on imaging done for
other reasons (Rossi 2018). People with urinary infections —
particularly pyelonephritis — or stones are imaged more. Hemminki 2017
showed this directly for urinary stones in Sweden: kidney cancer was the
most increased cancer after kidney/ureter stones, SIRs fell steeply with
follow-up time, and tumours were smaller, which the authors read as
surveillance bias. The Korean antibiotic study lists detection bias as a
limitation; Roderburg 2023 notes more physician contact among antibiotic
users.

**How it was handled:** time-window analyses (Søgaard, Jansåker,
Nørgaard), washouts (Park), and in Nørgaard 2018 stratification by
whether cystoscopy was done (kidney SIR 2.63 without vs 1.20 with
cystoscopy at 1–5 years). No study adjusted for imaging counts.

**Evidence from All of Us itself:** Wang 2025 found that UTI, kidney
stones and other diagnoses were all followed by more diagnoses of an
unrelated outcome, highest in the first year (e.g., after GI bleeding HR
2.56, 1.62–4.04, in All of Us in year 1 vs 2.14, 1.63–2.81, at 1–5
years). Russell 2018 (prostate) showed the same pattern for UTI and UTI
antibiotics: associations only 6–12 months before diagnosis. Mortensen
2026 argued that detection alone does not explain the year-1 kidney
excess because no compensatory deficit followed.

**Our interpretation:** adjust for (or match on) the number of abdominal
CT/ultrasound/MRI studies before time zero, and run an analysis
restricted to RCC found \>2 years after the last abdominal imaging;
stage/size would help but may be unavailable in All of Us EHR data \[NOT
CONFIRMED\].

## 5.2 Protopathic bias (early cancer treated as UTI)

Funston 2024 found UTI prescriptions start rising 9 months (5.3–12.7)
before renal cancer diagnosis. Jansåker 2025 and Søgaard 2019 found the
largest excesses within 3–6 months of the infection, and the Swedish
authors suggest occult cancers may present like cystitis. Boursi 2015
excluded antibiotic exposure in the year before diagnosis; Park used
1–5-year washouts; Huang used none.

**Our interpretation:** a 2-year lag is above every observed protopathic
window; the sensitivity analysis at 5 years tests the persistence seen
in Nørgaard's hematuria cohort (kidney SIR still 1.46 at ≥5 years per
medwireNews).

## 5.3 Confounding by indication

Antibiotics are given because of infection, so infection-related factors
(and the comorbidities that drive infections, such as diabetes, stones
and CKD) travel with antibiotic exposure. Boursi and Roderburg list it
as a limitation; Park adjusted for six infection categories; Huang
compared antibiotic durations within UTI patients and contrasted UTI
with pneumonia.

**Our interpretation:** the strongest design levers are active
comparators (class vs class within the same indication), the
recurrent-vs-single-UTI comparison, and an indication-specific analysis
(urinary vs non-urinary antibiotics).

## 5.4 Other

- **Recall bias** in self-reported exposures (Parker 2004; UK Biobank
  childhood antibiotics).

- **Exposure misclassification:** prescriptions ≠ ingestion (Park,
  Boursi); hospital antibiotics missing (Boursi). In All of Us, EHR
  coverage differs by site and over time \[NOT CONFIRMED\].

- **Outcome misclassification:** Read/ICD codes without pathology
  (Boursi, Huang); EHR and survey kidney-cancer cases overlap only
  partly in All of Us (313 in both of 1,266 EHR and 487 survey cases).

## 6.2 UTI and antibiotic prescribing patterns

- In US ambulatory visits 2009–2016, antibiotic prescribing was highest
  in Hispanic patients: 138.6 per 1,000 visits overall and 79.8 per
  1,000 inappropriate (Young 2023); the rate in White patients was 109
  per 1,000 (reported by MDedge).

- In KPSC women with cystitis, Hispanic women were slightly less likely
  than White women to develop recurrent UTI (aRR 0.93, 0.91–0.95)
  (Ackerson 2024).

- In All of Us, Hispanic participants were 18.8% of the first 315,000
  participants but 12.2% of EHR-captured cancers and 4.7% of
  survey-reported cancers (Aschebrook-Kilfoy 2022) — survey-based
  validation of cancer will be weaker for Hispanic participants.

- In All of Us, Hispanic patients admitted with community-acquired
  pneumonia were more likely than non-Hispanic White patients to receive
  guideline-concordant antibiotics (NHW vs Hispanic aOR 0.66, 0.48–0.91)
  (Gilmore 2023). Among 2,215 All of Us RCC patients, Hispanic patients
  more often reported being unable to afford medications (23.8% vs 8.2%
  White) (Agrawal 2026).

## 6.3 Any sign that either link differs by ethnicity?

None found. No UTI–kidney cancer or antibiotic–kidney cancer study
reported results by Hispanic ethnicity or Puerto Rican origin. The only
modifiers reported were sex and smoking (Parker 2004) and sex (Park
2024, stronger in women, P-interaction 0.026).

**Our interpretation:** effect modification by ethnicity is plausible
through differences in prescribing (more antibiotics overall),
obesity/diabetes/CKD prevalence, and access to imaging, but there is no
empirical signal to test against; this is a real gap, not just a
sparsity of papers, because island Puerto Ricans have kidney cancer
rates roughly half those of US Hispanics, which makes PR a natural
contrast if exposure patterns differ.

# 7. Mechanisms

## 7.1 Infection → kidney cancer

- **Chronic inflammation:** recurrent urinary infection is proposed to
  promote urologic cancer through chronic inflammation (stated as the
  rationale by Mortensen 2026 and Huang 2019).

- **Bacterial genotoxins:** uropathogenic E. coli can produce colibactin
  in the human urinary tract and caused DNA damage in bladder cells in a
  mouse UTI model (Chagneau 2021). Evidence is for urothelium;
  kidney-parenchyma (RCC) evidence is indirect. A rat study reporting
  renal genotoxic changes from colibactin/Cnf1 E. coli (Morgan 2019, Gut
  Pathog) was **retracted in 2026** and should not be cited.

- **Specific infection, specific tumour:** in Taiwan, urinary
  tuberculosis was associated with urothelial carcinoma (HR 3.38,
  2.01–5.69) but not RCC (2 of 1,287 vs 96 of 133,855; P = 0.240)
  (Lien 2013) — chronic urinary infection has a clearer link to
  urothelial than to renal-parenchymal cancer.

- **Kidney damage pathway (our interpretation):** pyelonephritis →
  scarring → CKD, an RCC risk factor (in Hispanic people OR 2.3, Hofmann
  2015); supports analysing pyelonephritis separately and treating CKD
  as a possible mediator.

## 7.2 Antibiotics → kidney cancer

- **Microbiome dysbiosis:** gut and urinary microbiota proposed as
  contributors to RCC (review, Yang 2023).

- **Adiposity:** adiposity mediated 4.3%–13.2% of the childhood
  antibiotic–kidney cancer association (Liu 2026).

- **Direct drug toxicity:** nitrofurantoin showed ‘some evidence’ of
  carcinogenic activity in male F344/N rats through uncommon kidney
  tubular cell neoplasms (NTP TR-341, 1989); IARC Vol 50 notes a
  hypothesis-generating human cohort for other sites.
  Fluoroquinolone-specific RCC mechanisms: no source found \[NOT
  CONFIRMED\].

## 7.3 Non-causal explanations

Reverse causation/protopathic bias and detection bias (Section 5). The
time pattern in Danish, Swedish and UK data — a large early excess that
declines — fits a marker effect better than a carcinogenic one, except
for the persistent late excess in women (Jansåker 2025; Mortensen 2026,
non-significant).

## 8.4 Events needed and detectable effect (our calculations)

Cox model, two-sided α = 0.05, power 80%, Schoenfeld formula: events D =
(1.96 + 0.84)² / \[p(1−p)(ln HR)²\], where p = share of the cohort
exposed.

| **Exposed share p**      | **HR 1.2** | **HR 1.3** | **HR 1.5** | **HR 2.0** |
|--------------------------|------------|------------|------------|------------|
| 5%                       | 4,971      | 2,401      | 1,005      | 344        |
| 10%                      | 2,624      | 1,267      | 530        | 182        |
| 20%                      | 1,476      | 713        | 298        | 102        |
| 50% (e.g., class A vs B) | 944        | 456        | 191        | 65         |

Minimum detectable HR for a given number of RCC events: 100 events →
2.54 (p = 10%) / 1.75 (p = 50%); 300 events → 1.71 / 1.38; 500 → 1.52 /
1.28; 1,000 → 1.34 / 1.19.

**Our interpretation:** effects in the literature after lags are
≈1.1–1.3, which need ≈700–2,600 events at realistic exposure shares.
Illustration only: 150,000 eligible participants × 5 years × 40 per
100,000 per year ≈ 300 RCC events (the eligible N and rate are
assumptions \[NOT CONFIRMED\] — compute both in the Researcher
Workbench). That supports detecting HR ≈1.7 for recurrent UTI (p ≈ 10%)
but not HR 1.2–1.3, and a Hispanic subgroup (≈19% of participants) would
have ≈50–60 events. Plan the ethnicity analysis as exploratory, consider
combining cystitis/pyelonephritis for the primary test, and use courses
as a continuous trend term to gain power.

# 9. Gaps and what our study would add

1.  **No study separates infection from antibiotic for RCC.** Why it
    matters: if antibiotics contribute, stewardship is a prevention
    lever; if it is infection or detection, it is not. Our
    class-within-indication and recurrent-vs-single-UTI contrasts
    address this directly.

2.  **The only recurrent-UTI study (Mortensen 2026) is hospital-based
    and compares with the general population, without antibiotic,
    smoking or BMI data.** Why it matters: its non-significant late
    excess (SIR 1.24; women 1.39) cannot distinguish a small true effect
    from residual confounding; an internal-comparator EHR cohort can.

3.  **Cystitis vs pyelonephritis has never been compared within one
    study.** Why it matters: pyelonephritis is the plausible route to
    kidney parenchyma and the strongest trigger for imaging, so the
    contrast speaks to mechanism vs detection.

4.  **No US EHR, multi-ethnic or Hispanic/Puerto Rican estimates.** Why
    it matters: Hispanic patients receive more antibiotics per
    ambulatory visit, have a stronger CKD–RCC association and higher US
    RCC incidence, while island Puerto Ricans have much lower kidney
    cancer rates.

5.  **No validated US EHR algorithm for incident RCC.** Why it matters:
    outcome misclassification biases toward the null and distorts
    subgroup comparisons; our study can report concordance with
    procedure codes and survey self-report.

6.  **Imaging intensity is never adjusted for, although detection bias
    has been shown in All of Us data (Wang 2025).** Why it matters: it
    is the main measurable driver of early excess risk.
